# Methodology

## 3.1 Research Design

This study follows a replication-first methodology, in which the objective is to faithfully reproduce a previously published biological age algorithm — the Phenotypic Age measure introduced by Levine et al. (2018) — using contemporary National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data, before any methodological modification or extension is considered. Every implementation decision in this study was required to be supported by documented evidence, drawn either from the original methodological publications, official NHANES/CDC laboratory documentation, or independently validated third-party software implementations. Where evidence was insufficient to support a decision outright, the decision was either deferred, adopted provisionally with an explicitly documented rationale, or escalated for direct verification against primary sources.

This governance-first approach was adopted deliberately. Biological age algorithms such as Phenotypic Age are frequently reimplemented across studies without independent verification of underlying assumptions — biomarker units, laboratory comparability across survey cycles, or missing-data handling — introducing a risk that seemingly minor implementation choices silently distort the resulting estimates. Accordingly, this study treats methodological transparency as a primary research output, not merely a preliminary step toward implementation.

## 3.2 Data Source and Study Population

The Phenotypic Age algorithm was originally derived and trained on NHANES III (1988–1994), a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of the United States civilian population conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). NHANES III remains the training reference for the algorithm's coefficients in this study; it is not used as a target for score computation.

For the replication and application of Phenotypic Age to contemporary data, this study targets three continuous NHANES survey cycles: 2015–2016, 2017–2018, and the pre-pandemic 2017–March 2020 combined cycle. This cycle range was selected to prioritize data recency while excluding the 2011–2014 cycles, which — as established during the data harmonization phase of this study — did not include C-reactive protein (CRP) measurements, one of the nine biomarkers required by the Phenotypic Age formula. Selecting a cycle range in which all required biomarkers are consistently available across the full target window avoided the need for cycle-specific biomarker substitution or exclusion, consistent with the principle of methodological simplicity favored throughout this study.

## 3.3 Biomarker Selection and the Phenotypic Age Algorithm

Phenotypic Age is a composite biological age estimate derived from nine routine clinical biomarkers together with chronological age: albumin, creatinine, fasting glucose, C-reactive protein (log-transformed), lymphocyte percentage, mean cell volume, red cell distribution width, alkaline phosphatase, and white blood cell count. These biomarkers were originally selected by Levine et al. (2018) from a candidate panel of forty-two NHANES III clinical measures, using an elastic-net-penalized Cox proportional hazards model for ten-year all-cause mortality, with the final biomarker set determined via ten-fold cross-validation.

The nine selected biomarkers and chronological age were subsequently combined in a two-stage parametric procedure. First, a Gompertz proportional hazards model was fit to estimate each individual's ten-year mortality risk based on a linear combination of the biomarkers (the "linear predictor," denoted *xb*). Second, this mortality risk was re-expressed in chronological-age-equivalent units — Phenotypic Age — using the parameters of an age-only Gompertz model as a reference scale.

A methodologically significant complication arose during the verification phase of this study: the equation as originally printed in Levine et al. (2018) was found to omit a necessary computational step, rendering it unresolvable as published. This was independently confirmed via a formal correction subsequently issued for a companion validation study by an overlapping author group (Liu et al., 2018; correction published 2019), which supplied the complete, resolvable form of the equation, including the model intercept and Gompertz distribution parameters. This study adopted the corrected formulation as canonical, a decision supported not only by the direct erratum but by convergent evidence from an independent third-party software implementation (the BioAge R package) and multiple independent secondary reconstructions that reproduce the identical coefficient values. This convergence across independent sources — rather than reliance on the original publication alone — was treated as satisfying this study's evidentiary threshold for adopting a coefficient set with high confidence.

## 3.4 Measurement Unit Standardization

A critical methodological finding emerged during direct verification of the source publication against the NHANES variable documentation. Levine et al. (2018) specify the Phenotypic Age formula's biomarker coefficients in Système International (SI) units — grams per liter for albumin, micromoles per liter for creatinine, and millimoles per liter for glucose — as confirmed independently in both the article's main-text table and its supplementary methodological materials. NHANES, by contrast, reports these same biomarkers in United States conventional clinical units by default (grams per deciliter for albumin; milligrams per deciliter for creatinine and glucose). Applying the published coefficients directly to unconverted NHANES values, without unit standardization, would produce systematically and substantially incorrect Phenotypic Age estimates, given that the two unit systems for creatinine alone differ by a multiplicative factor of approximately 88.4.

This study therefore applies an explicit unit-standardization step prior to formula evaluation: albumin values are multiplied by 10 (converting g/dL to g/L), creatinine values are multiplied by 88.4 (mg/dL to µmol/L), and glucose values are multiplied by 0.0555 (mg/dL to mmol/L). C-reactive protein required particular care: the source publication specifies its unit as milligrams per deciliter, an unconventional choice for high-sensitivity CRP, which NHANES reports in milligrams per liter. This unit was independently corroborated across two separately typeset tables in the source publication and its supplementary materials, which was judged sufficient to rule out a transcription artifact; CRP values are accordingly divided by 10 prior to the natural-log transformation the formula requires. The remaining five biomarkers (lymphocyte percentage, mean cell volume, red cell distribution width, alkaline phosphatase, and white blood cell count) required no conversion, as NHANES's native reporting units match the formula's specification directly.

This finding illustrates a broader methodological point relevant to replication studies of clinical biomarker algorithms: the reuse of a published formula's coefficients is not, by itself, sufficient for faithful replication. The measurement-unit convention under which those coefficients were estimated must be independently verified against the specific dataset to which they are subsequently applied, since unit mismatches of this kind are not detectable through inspection of the coefficient values alone.

## 3.5 Cross-Cycle Laboratory Harmonization

Because NHANES periodically updates its clinical laboratory instrumentation, protocols, and contracted laboratories, biomarker values measured in different survey cycles are not automatically comparable on a common scale. This study conducted a systematic review of NCHS laboratory documentation for each of the nine required biomarkers across the three target cycles to identify any such discontinuities.

Two of the nine biomarkers required correction. The C-reactive protein assay transitioned between the 2015–2016 cycle (measured in-house on Beckman Coulter instrumentation) and the 2017–2018 and 2017–March 2020 cycles (measured at an external contract laboratory on different instrumentation). NCHS conducted and published an official method-validation ("bridging") study comparing the two measurement approaches on a shared sample of specimens, and released regression equations to adjust values from one measurement scale onto the other; this study adopts those published equations, applied in the direction of aligning earlier-cycle values onto the later, currently ongoing measurement scale. An analogous instrument transition was identified for the standard biochemistry panel — which includes albumin, creatinine, and alkaline phosphatase — between the same cycle boundary, and NCHS similarly published bridging equations for this panel, which this study adopts under the same directional convention.

The remaining biomarkers — fasting glucose and the four components drawn from the complete blood count panel (lymphocyte percentage, mean cell volume, red cell distribution width, and white blood cell count) — were confirmed, via direct inspection of NCHS's own cycle-specific laboratory documentation, to have used identical instrumentation and measurement methods across all three target cycles, requiring no cross-cycle adjustment.

A residual, lower-priority methodological question remains regarding creatinine specifically: published calibration research (Selvin et al., 2007) demonstrated that NHANES III and the earliest continuous NHANES cycles (1999–2000) exhibited a measurable positive bias in creatinine values relative to a standardized enzymatic reference method, a bias not observed in subsequent cycles studied. Because the Phenotypic Age coefficients were originally estimated on NHANES III data — the biased side of this documented discrepancy — a theoretical question remains as to whether the original coefficients implicitly absorb this historical bias in a manner that could interact with modern, unbiased creatinine measurements. This question is treated as a residual sensitivity consideration for the validation phase of this study rather than a blocking methodological concern, since the calibration study in question did not examine cycles beyond 2003–2004 and therefore cannot directly confirm or rule out continued bias in the cycles this study targets.

## 3.6 Missing Data Handling

Consistent with the approach taken in the original Phenotypic Age derivation, this study applies complete-case exclusion: a participant's record is excluded from Phenotypic Age computation if any of the nine required biomarkers is missing. This decision favors methodological consistency with the original algorithm's derivation over the additional complexity of imputation, though it is recognized as a provisional choice pending an empirical check — conducted during the validation phase — of whether missingness across the nine biomarkers is plausibly random, or instead concentrated in identifiable subpopulations in a manner that could bias the resulting analytic sample.

## 3.7 Analytic Pipeline

The end-to-end computational procedure proceeds through seven sequential stages: (1) acquisition of the relevant NHANES demographic and laboratory data files for each target cycle; (2) extraction of the nine biomarker variables and chronological age, with attention to selecting the correct fasting-subsample glucose variable rather than the non-fasting biochemistry-panel alternative; (3) unit standardization, as described in Section 3.4; (4) cross-cycle bridging adjustment, as described in Section 3.5; (5) complete-case exclusion of records with any missing biomarker; (6) evaluation of the Phenotypic Age formula; and (7) a set of output validation checks, described below.

## 3.8 Validation Strategy

Four complementary validation checks are specified for the resulting Phenotypic Age estimates. First, a face-validity check assesses whether Phenotypic Age correlates with chronological age at a magnitude consistent with an independent third-party software implementation of the same algorithm, computed on the identical extracted sample — this comparative approach was adopted in preference to an absolute correlation threshold, since no independently verified benchmark correlation coefficient specific to the corrected formula and the target NHANES cycles could be located in the literature. Second, a cross-implementation agreement check compares this study's output directly against the BioAge R package's own computation of Phenotypic Age, providing an indirect but methodologically informative check on whether the coefficients adopted in this study match those embedded in an independently maintained implementation. Third, a cross-cycle bridging effectiveness check compares the distribution of age-adjusted Phenotypic Age residuals before and after the harmonization adjustments described in Section 3.5, to confirm that the applied corrections measurably reduce — rather than merely presume to address — any cross-cycle discontinuity. Fourth, a missingness-pattern check assesses whether biomarker missingness within the analytic sample is consistent with a missing-completely-at-random assumption, informing whether the complete-case exclusion approach adopted in Section 3.6 remains appropriate.

## 3.9 Limitations

This study is scoped as a faithful replication of an existing algorithm rather than the development or external validation of a novel biological age measure; accordingly, it does not undertake independent validation against morbidity or mortality outcomes, of the kind originally reported by Levine et al. (2018) and subsequently confirmed in an independent NHANES IV cohort by Liu et al. (2018). Two methodological questions remain open at a level classified, in this study's internal evidentiary framework, as non-blocking: direct confirmation of the exact coefficient values embedded in the BioAge package's source code (relied upon here only indirectly, through output convergence), and the residual creatinine calibration question described in Section 3.5. Both are addressed as sensitivity considerations during the validation phase rather than treated as resolved with certainty.
